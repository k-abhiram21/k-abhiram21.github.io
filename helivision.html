EMBEDDED VISION / TEAM PROJECT

Bring the missed
vehicle into view.

HeliVision turns camera detections into simple visual and spoken cues. It began with seeing how partial vision loss can make road navigation harder, and became a working camera-to-cue helmet prototype.

My part

Built with

The idea ↓How it works ↓Building it ↓
02 WHY THIS IDEA

Help notice what’s
easy to miss.

The idea began with a real situation: seeing how partial vision loss from optic nerve damage could make it difficult to notice vehicles approaching from one side, especially at night. A person could be looking at the road and still miss part of what was happening around them.

I wondered whether a helmet-mounted camera could notice those vehicles and draw attention to their position. Instead of showing another full video feed, it would turn detections into simple spatial cues on a small display, paired with audio.

That question became HeliVision. I proposed the concept, then worked through the software and integration with my teammates.

04 LEARNING WHILE BUILDING

The real work was
between the components.

Getting from a camera frame to a useful cue meant learning the Raspberry Pi ecosystem while building with it. Camera interfaces, OS versions, Python dependencies, and the display each brought their own assumptions.

I adapted the camera and display stack, separated frame capture from inference, and added automatic image-history management for the Pi’s limited storage. Those changes helped turn individual components into a working pipeline.

The prototype combined local inference, coordinate mapping, and multimodal feedback. In basic outdoor checks, it detected both parked and moving vehicles.

I led the software and debugging, using LLM tools along the way. Hardware integration was shared with teammates.

Inside the implementation ↓

Resolve the camera stack first.

Part of the debugging was finding which layer was failing. The older camera path depended on legacy Raspberry Pi libraries; the final capture service uses Picamera2. Getting the OS, camera interface, and Python environment to agree gave the detector a dependable input to work with.

Separate capture from inference.

camera_service.py updates a shared latest-frame image. vehicle_detection.py consumes that input independently and runs the TensorFlow Lite detector. This keeps the handoff simple: the camera supplies a recent view without building an ever-growing archive of input frames.

Translate detections into positions.

The detector takes each bounding box’s centre, maps it into the OLED’s coordinate space, and groups it into horizontal regions. Small marks on the display, directional indicators, and spoken alerts give the detection a position the rider can understand. Moving from the text LCD to the OLED also made that spatial representation possible.

Make the pipeline manageable on the Pi.

A separate cleanup service prunes recent saved detection images. Those images remain useful for debugging, while automatic history management limits their storage footprint during a run. Keeping capture, detection, and cleanup as separate services made it easier to locate problems during integration.
