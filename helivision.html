02 WHY THIS IDEA

Where the idea came from.

I’d seen how partial vision loss from optic nerve damage could make vehicles on one side difficult to notice, especially at night. It made me wonder whether a camera could help draw attention to them.

My idea was to put that camera on a helmet and turn detections into a few simple cues: vehicle positions on a small display, with audio alongside them.

I proposed the concept and led the coding and debugging. We built it as a team, sharing the hardware work.

03 FROM SCENE TO CUE

From a camera frame
to a display cue.

The camera feeds a vehicle detector. Its output is mapped onto an OLED at the lower edge of the visor and paired with audio cues.

Front view · OLED at the lower centre of the visor

01 / CAPTURE

Capture the frame.

A dedicated camera process supplies the latest view to the detector.

02 / INTERPRET

Find the vehicles.

TensorFlow Lite detections are mapped to horizontal regions and display coordinates.

03 / COMMUNICATE

Show where they are.

The OLED, directional indicators, and spoken alerts translate the output into feedback.

04 LEARNING WHILE BUILDING

Getting the parts
to work together.

I was learning the Raspberry Pi ecosystem while building the prototype. We ran into OS and Python library compatibility issues, and the first text LCD didn’t work with our setup.

We switched to a small OLED, which let us show vehicle positions. I also separated camera capture from detection and added image cleanup so saved outputs wouldn’t fill the Pi’s storage.

We carried the helmet on a road walk to try it outdoors. It detected parked vehicles as well as vehicles passing by.

I wrote most of the code and handled most of the debugging, with help from LLM tools. My teammates and I shared the hardware connections and assembly.

Inside the implementation ↓

Getting a camera feed.

The camera code initially depended on older Raspberry Pi libraries. I worked through the OS and Python compatibility issues and moved the final capture service to Picamera2. That gave us a recent frame the detector could read.

The handoff to the detector.

camera_service.py updates a shared latest-frame image. vehicle_detection.py reads it independently and runs TensorFlow Lite inference. Overwriting that input kept capture separate from detection without continuously saving every incoming frame.

Mapping a vehicle onto the display.

I used the centre of each detected bounding box to map vehicles into the OLED’s coordinates and horizontal regions. The display marks their positions, while directional indicators and spoken alerts provide additional cues. The OLED made this spatial view possible.

Keeping saved images under control.

Detection outputs were useful while debugging, but we couldn’t keep saving them indefinitely. I added a cleanup service that keeps a recent buffer and deletes older outputs. Having capture, detection, and cleanup in separate services also made it easier to find where something had gone wrong.
