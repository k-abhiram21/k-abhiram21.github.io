02 LLMLENS / PHASE 1

CHARACTERIZING DETECTION LIMITS

Confidence is a signal.
How far does it get us?

I wanted to ask how far uncertainty-based hallucination detection can go, beyond developing another detector.

Working across model families and tasks, we studied whether signals from a model’s sampled outputs distinguish correct answers from hallucinations.

The key distinction is simple: answers can agree with one another and still be wrong. My work helped characterize where these uncertainty signals remain useful and where that separation weakens.

MY CONTRIBUTION

Research question, methodology, software, formal analysis, and manuscript writing, alongside the team.

MANUSCRIPT · WITH EDITOR AT KAIS

Where Internal Signals Fail: Characterizing the Performance Ceiling of Uncertainty-Based Hallucination Detection

Submitted to Knowledge and Information Systems.

How I approached the investigation ↓

Make the question precise.

I was interested in the reach of uncertainty as a signal: when does it help us judge an answer, and when does it become less informative? Framing that question shaped the work before choosing how to measure it.

Connect the question to an investigation.

I worked on the methodology, software, and analysis with the team. That meant turning a broad idea about reliability into experiments we could examine, comparing behaviour across different settings, and working through what the results actually supported.

Make the explanation as careful as the code.

Manuscript writing was part of the technical work. I helped connect the motivation, experimental reasoning, and findings into a clear argument, so readers could understand both the question and the evidence behind our conclusions.

03 MY ROUTING WORK / PHASE 2

MAKING MORE CONSIDERED MODEL DECISIONS

A specialist is relevant.
Should we use its answer?

I work on the routing layer: deciding when a different model response is worth using.

The broader research framework is a team effort. My contribution centres on routing, implementation and debugging, verification, and evaluation—turning research questions into behaviour we can inspect.

The question is practical: adding another model or component should earn its place. I’m interested in when an intervention helps, how to understand its failures, and what makes the resulting system more dependable.

MY CONTRIBUTION / ROUTING

Research engineering across the routing layer: implementation, debugging, verification work, and evaluation tooling, alongside the team’s wider research effort.

How I shaped the routing question ↓

Start with the decision that matters.

A model being relevant to a topic does not automatically make its response more useful. I focused on the choice the system ultimately has to make: whether an intervention is worth using for the problem in front of it.

Translate the idea into engineering work.

My contribution spans routing implementation, debugging, verification, and evaluation. I work between the research question and the code, checking whether behaviour matches the intention and making the implementation easier to reason about when it does not.

Own a clear part of a shared effort.

The wider research framework is built by the team. My responsibility is the routing work within it, and connecting that work to the surrounding system. That requires understanding neighbouring components while keeping my own contribution concrete and testable.

What debugging taught me about model decisions ↓

Look beyond a convincing response.

Working on the decision layer kept bringing me back to the gap between plausibility and usefulness. Something can look appropriate at first glance and still behave differently from what the task needs. I investigate those gaps rather than treating a convincing output as the end of the work.

Follow the behaviour back to its cause.

I trace how a decision is reached, inspect the relevant implementation, and check the assumptions around it. Making failures understandable helps me decide what to change and gives the next experiment a more specific purpose.

Carry the lesson into the next iteration.

My coding and verifier work feed back into the research: what should count as useful evidence, what deserves another check, and how can a decision be easier to inspect? Those questions connect debugging with the larger problem of reliable AI behaviour.

How I make the work easier to evaluate ↓

Keep the question connected to the result.

I work on the evaluation code as well as the routing implementation. A result is more useful when we can explain what was compared, what changed, and why that comparison answers the original question.

Check the surrounding engineering.

Part of my contribution is tightening checks and debugging the machinery used to study model behaviour. This work makes experiments easier to inspect and helps distinguish an implementation issue from a research finding.

Consider the system as a whole.

I am interested in whether an added component earns its place: does it help with the problem, what does it cost to use, and how does it fit with the rest of the system? That perspective keeps the research connected to practical engineering decisions.
